# Teaching Practice

Load this file during every lesson-design pass. It converts the lesson contract in `SKILL.md` into operational checks; it does not replace mission, learner-evidence, source, workspace, or acceptance ownership in Teach.

## Design the learning sequence

1. **Check prerequisites before asking for unsupported problem solving.** Identify the concepts and procedures the learner must already know. For an unknown prerequisite, model the smallest useful worked example or guided demonstration, make the reasoning visible, and then ask the learner to perform a nearby step with less support. Do not treat unassisted struggle with unknown prerequisites as useful difficulty. Worked examples improved novice performance in the algebra tasks studied by Sweller and Cooper; that bounded result supports initial guidance, not a universal script for every learner or domain ([Sweller & Cooper, 1985](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3)).

2. **Require retrieval before reveal.** Ask the learner to recall, explain, choose, or apply the target without copying and before showing the answer or inviting rereading. Reveal guidance after the attempt, then require another unaided use. Schedule important ideas for retrieval again in later lessons; repeated retrieval, rather than repeated study after successful recall, improved delayed vocabulary retention in the experiments reported by Karpicke and Roediger ([Karpicke & Roediger, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)).

3. **Space later retrieval according to the mission.** Put future retrieval far enough away that it is not a copy of immediate performance, but soon enough to support the learner's required retention horizon. Record the future retrieval target in the lesson or next-step decision. Do not impose a universal interval: useful spacing varied with final-test delay in Cepeda and colleagues' study ([Cepeda et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)).

4. **Interleave only founded, related skills.** Mix problems when the learner already knows the basics of each included method and must learn when to select among them. Keep an unknown prerequisite blocked or model it first. Rohrer and Taylor found benefits from shuffled mathematics practice after the relevant methods had been introduced; their experiments do not establish that mixing unfamiliar prerequisites is beneficial ([Rohrer & Taylor, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8)).

5. **Calibrate useful effort.** Make the task hard enough to require retrieval or judgment, but keep a known prerequisite path, a realistic chance of success, and available feedback. If failure exposes an unknown prerequisite, stop escalating difficulty, model the missing step, and retry a nearby task. Difficulty alone is not evidence of learning.

6. **Make feedback corrective and actionable.** State whether the attempt met the success criterion, identify the relevant gap, and give a next action or retry. Prefer prompt and automatic feedback when correctness can be judged reliably, but choose timing for the task rather than asserting that immediate feedback is always best. In Butler, Karpicke, and Roediger's multiple-choice experiments, corrective feedback aided learning and delayed feedback outperformed immediate feedback on the delayed test ([Butler, Karpicke & Roediger, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.13.4.273)).

7. **Remove answer cues.** Make success depend on the target knowledge or skill, not option formatting, answer length, grammatical fit, testwise elimination, or accidental visual emphasis. Keep comparable options parallel and plausible when options are necessary; for free response, hide the reveal until after commitment. This is an operational paraphrase of the NBME's official guidance on technical flaws and cues, not a copied item-writing template ([NBME Item-Writing Guide, October 2024](https://info.nbme.org/rs/552-QHC-046/images/NBME_Item-Writing-Guide.pdf?version=0)).

## Accept the lesson

Do not accept the lesson until every item passes:

- [ ] **Mission-linked win:** the learner can name the one tangible outcome and how it advances the mission.
- [ ] **Prerequisite safety:** every required prerequisite is evidenced as known or receives minimal worked guidance before unsupported performance.
- [ ] **Retrieval before reveal:** at least one prompt requires unaided recall or application before rereading, hints, or answer display.
- [ ] **Relevant practice:** the learner performs the target skill rather than only reading about it.
- [ ] **Corrective feedback:** the practice yields a correctness signal, a specific correction where needed, and an actionable retry; its timing fits the task.
- [ ] **Observable success:** the lesson states what successful performance looks like and lets the learner demonstrate it.
- [ ] **Trustworthy citations:** substantive factual claims have adjacent approved citations; exercises and hypotheses are labelled.
- [ ] **Primary-source recommendation:** the ending points to one approved primary source for deeper study.
- [ ] **Follow-up:** the ending invites questions or reports of difficulty.
- [ ] **Reusable assets:** existing assets and references were inspected, applicable ones are reused, and new reusable behavior has one shared home.
- [ ] **No answer clues:** formatting, length, wording, option structure, and reveal behavior do not disclose the answer.
- [ ] **Future retrieval:** an important idea is selected for later unaided retrieval without claiming a universal schedule.

## Evidence and limits

These sources justify bounded design actions, not a universal teaching formula. Lesson-specific factual content still requires the source process and approved citations owned by Teach and `research`.

| Karpicke & Roediger, “The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning” | *Science* 319 (2008), DOI [`10.1126/science.1152408`](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408) | Repeated retrieval supported delayed retention in the reported foreign-language vocabulary experiments. | Does not prescribe one retrieval format, count, or schedule for every domain. |
| Cepeda et al., “Spacing Effects in Learning: A Temporal Ridgeline of Optimal Retention” | *Psychological Science* 19 (2008), DOI [`10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x`](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x) | Review-gap effectiveness depended on the final retention interval in a study of more than 1,350 participants with delays up to one year. | Does not supply a universal spacing interval. |
